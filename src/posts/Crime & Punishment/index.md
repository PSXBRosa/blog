Back in January, as a resolution for the year of 2026, I set the goal of reading one great novel every month. And now, by the end of [may](#comment:A%20total%20of%20six%20months,%20if%20you're%20still%20learning%20how%20to%20count), I have the pleasure to announce for all who wish to listen that I have most efficiently finished my [first book of the year](#comment:I%20actually%20read%20a%20couple%20of%20silly%20YA%20novels%20during%20these%20past%20few%20months,%20but%20they%20barely%20merit%20these%20parenthesis.)

I didn't know much about Dostoyevsky when I got this book from my parents' bookshelf. I had bought the novel at a book fair nine years ago with some other impulse buys, most of which I never tried to read or were too boring to finish. And to be frank, I didn't give much thought to the choice. I had heard of its name before and it had been sitting on a dusty shelf for almost a decade.

I'd love to tell you that it was an instant page turner, but you already know that it took me six months to finish the book. Blaming my recent move to France and losing the book at the airport would be convincing excuses. But I don't want to betray some of the main themes of the book, so I gotta be honest. The real reason is that I'm struggling to stick to the goals I set for myself. Between Instagram reels and AI chatbots, I've been feeling like the shittiest manager at the terrible fast food joint I call life. But! Very much like in the book, I think that I'm finding a quiet form of salvation in the end.

Crime and Punishment follows the mental gymnastics of [Rodion Raskolnikov](#comment:Russian%20literature%20revealed%20to%20me%20how%20easy%20it%20is%20to%20identify%20names%20without%20ever%20really%20reading%20them), as he gaslights himself into believing that the only way out of his shitty situation as a broke student is through the assassination of the pawnbroker who usually lends him money. Using his utilitarian philosophy, he concludes that the world would probably be better off without that old hag anyways and that he really could use her coins. Add to the recipe the pressure to be a provider for his family, a bit of pride and some [edgy worldviews](#comment:%20The%20wolf%20doesn't%20concern%20itself%20with%20morals) and you get the first part of the book's title.

It took me a while to understand how I was supposed to read this novel. I saw somewhere at the beginning of my readings that Dostoyevsky uses his stories to debate philosophy, and I never read philosophy either. Rodion doesn't do much to help the story stay interesting, as he spends most of the narrative acting crazy and erratic after [killing the old pawn-lady](#comment:Spoiler%20Warning!). 

But slowly I started to understand the smart tactics used by the narrative to get its points across. Almost every character is a mirror pointed at Raskolnikov and his “acting crazy” is a direct consequence of having to confront the same ideas he used to justify murder, now turned against people he cares about, or of having to watch how other characters, often worse off than him, find alternatives to their problems that do not require an axe.

Ultimately, what shocked me most is how [fresh](#comment:Published%20in%201866) the novel still feels: the characters, the struggles, the ideologies being mocked, even the narrative devices. And among the many stabs this book takes at society back then, I picked out a few that stood out to me as especially relevant today. I present to you bellow [my attempts](#comment:This%20is%20not%20the%20best%20place%20to%20get%20your%20literature%20reviews%20btw) to transport Russian wisdom from a century and a half ago to my everyday issues.

----- 

1. **Environmental determinism:** This is basically a fancy way of asking: can we blame society for our own bad choices? The book's answer is a resounding **no**. While the suffocating, poverty-stricken environment of St. Petersburg practically breeds crime, the text completely rejects the excuse that "the world made me do it". Sure, our surroundings shape us, but we aren't automatically forgiven by them. It's incredibly tempting to blame our collapsing attention spans on [addictive algorithms and constant feeds](#comment:Thanks%20for%20the%20brain%20rot), but the novel insists that even inside a broken environment, we still retain some agency over who we choose to become.

2. **Nihilism:** Perhaps the clearest antagonist in the book besides [society](#comment:A%20Joker%20reference%20from%20the%201800s?) is Svidrigailov. If every character is a twisted reflection of Rodion, Svidrigailov is what he would become if he succeeded in discarding all his morals and proved himself an [_extraordinary man_](#comment:Dostoyevsky's%20term%20for%20an%20actual%20psychopath). If nothing matters, if the wicked don't get punished, if all we have is here, now and ourselves, why should we care about morals and about those around us? Why not just optimize for pure self-interest? Dostoyevsky shows that fully committing to this mindset doesn't make you a god, it just leaves you spiritually detached and hollow. It feels relevant today, where so much of modern life can feel transactional, ironic, or optimized for attention. But the book argues that we find meaning not by escaping our burdens, but by actively choosing to carry them.

3. **The strange comfort of self-pity:** Raskolnikov is presented with many exits early on. Friends try to help him and opportunities pop up, but he repeatedly retreats into his tiny room to [marinate in his own misery](#comment:Doomscrolling) . He eventually confesses to Sonya that he had grown attached to his suffering. There is a weird comfort in self-pity . Sometimes we cling to our struggles because they give us a perfect excuse for doing nothing. It’s way easier to endlessly analyze our problems than to actually act, which is exactly how Raskolnikov spends most of the novel: paralyzed between massive theories and complete inertia.

4. **Hiding behind empty ideologies:** The book is full of characters hiding behind ready-made systems of thought. Ideologies get dangerous when they become an excuse to stop thinking and avoid personal responsibility . By outsourcing every moral choice to a [political, intellectual or religious rulebook](#comment:Or%20an%20all%20knowing%20oracle), these characters convince themselves that whatever they do is justified because they're just "following the script". They are usually quick to bend those exact principles whenever it’s personally convenient, all while trying to look righteous. This also doesn't sound out of place in 2026. People today often wear beliefs like fashion choices or team sports, complex moral questions get reduced into simple slogans and aesthetics. An ideology is only as valuable as the honesty of the person following it; otherwise, it’s just a fancy tool for self-deception.

There are many other themes in this book that I could talk about, like self-sacrifice, honesty, community, charity, and others, but if you're interested you can [look for it somewhere else](#comment:Or%20get%20off%20your%20phone%20and%20read%20the%20book!).
